Let's start with something you have probably done. On the left, I ask for a piece on how optimism improves well-being. The model doesn't ask anything. It writes a long text, the tone is wrong, I correct it, it writes another long text, and we go back and forth. This example comes from Figure 2 of the paper. On the right is the other strategy: I write a mini-specification up front, with length, tone, focus, examples and structure. Then I still have to tweak the result, and we go round again. This one is our own illustration, not from the paper. Click: now replace that strategy with CollabLLM. Same vague request, but the model asks first: what tone, and which angle, resilience or relationships? The user answers in one line, and the model proposes an opening paragraph and suggests adding anecdotes. Click: the paper's evaluation of this example. On the left the user is frustrated, quality is low at BLEU 0.32 and an LLM judge rates interactivity 0.2. On the right the user finds it helpful and efficient, BLEU is 0.46 and interactivity is 0.8. Note that the figure prints the same 1.39k tokens read for both sides. So why don't today's models do this by default?

Why does this happen, and how do the authors fix it?

Fig. 2 (training panels), p. 2; Sec. 1 & 3, p. 1–3

The reason is how models are trained. Click: here is standard RLHF: we take a context x and a response y, rate the response at the next turn with thumbs up or down, and train the policy on these single-turn rewards. Under that signal, a clarifying question looks worse than a full answer, because it doesn't give the user anything yet. The paper's point is that summing good single-turn rewards does not guarantee a good conversation overall. Click: CollabLLM changes the reward. It takes the current model turn, simulates the future turns with a user simulator, and scores the response by where the conversation ends up. This is called the Multiturn-aware Reward, and the model is fine-tuned on it with RL. Yash will now show how this reward is defined and computed.
