Thanks, Iuliia. I will focus on the authors' experiments. They test the same 8B model family in three settings. Click: document editing uses 100 Medium articles, with BLEU measuring how closely the final document matches the reference. Click: coding uses 600 problems and the pass rate of the final code. Click: math uses 200 difficult, level-five problems, with an LLM judge checking the final answer. GPT-4o-mini plays the user and reveals the goal through a conversation. The comparisons are Base, Proactive Base, which receives clarification instructions, and four CollabLLM variants. These are simulated interactions. I will show the separate study with real people next.

Every trained variant improves task scores over both baselines

PPO leads in writing and coding.
Online DPO leads in math.

Table 1, p. 6 (model rows)

This is the original results table. The green rows are the two baselines, and the orange rows are the trained variants. For now, look at BLEU, pass rate and accuracy. Proactive prompting already helps, but each trained variant scores above both baselines on all three task metrics. Click: there is no single winner across every column. PPO has the highest writing score, 38.5, and coding pass rate, 14.0. Online DPO reaches 16.5 percent accuracy in math, compared with 12.5 for Proactive Base. That is four percentage points, or 32 percent relative improvement. Absolute math accuracy is still low. The paper gives no confidence intervals here, so I treat these as reported score differences rather than proof that every gap is statistically reliable.

Online DPO uses fewer tokens and is more interactive

Table 1, p. 6; ITR divided by 100

Now I compare Online DPO with Proactive Base on the other two objectives. On the left, lower bars mean less assistant text for users to read. Tokens fall on every task. In math, for example, the conversation drops from 2.90 to 2.37 thousand tokens. This measures reading load, not training cost or response latency. On the right, higher bars mean more interactivity according to the Claude-3.5-Sonnet judge. Writing rises from 0.62 to 0.92, coding from 0.337 to 0.52, and math from 0.46 to 0.60. I show the task-level scores because the headline percentage depends on the judge's rescaling. Yash will explain that limitation in his analysis.

Real users give higher ratings and spend less time

201 participants · anonymous assistants

Fig. 7(a–c), p. 8; Sec. 6, p. 7–8

Do these gains also appear with real people? The authors recruited 201 participants for writing tasks. Each person used an anonymous assistant for at least eight turns and rated the resulting document and the interaction on a one-to-ten scale. Within each panel, the order is Base, Proactive Base, then CollabLLM. Document quality rises from 8.16 to 8.50, and interaction quality from 8.11 to 8.60. These are modest gains in the average ratings. The third panel shows time: 1,237 seconds with Base versus 1,108 with CollabLLM, a 10.4 percent reduction. These results support usefulness on this writing task, but do not establish the same benefit for coding or math with real users.

Clarification transfers to QA, with more unnecessary questions

Coding-trained model · zero-shot Abg-CoQA

Table 2, p. 7

The authors also test transfer. They take the Online DPO model trained on coding and evaluate it on Abg-CoQA without further training. Here, success means choosing the right action: ask for clarification when a question is ambiguous, or answer directly when it is clear. Click: CollabLLM asks on 52.84 percent of ambiguous questions, compared with 16.26 for Base. But the second group shows the trade-off. It answers clear questions directly only 72.32 percent of the time, below Base's 90.40. Macro accuracy improves to 62.58 percent, although macro F1 stays below GPT-4o. So the behaviour transfers, but the model still needs to learn when a question is actually useful.

Both models pass 99.7% of safety checks

80 adversarial queries · four evaluators per response

Appendix C, p. 16–17; Table 8, p. 17

Finally, could stronger collaboration help a user with harmful intentions? The authors start from a safety-aligned base model and check whether collaborative fine-tuning weakens that alignment. They test 80 adversarial queries, 20 from each harm category, using the Azure AI Evaluation SDK. Every response receives four harm evaluations. Both models pass 319 of the 320 checks, which rounds to 99.7 percent. The table shows small differences in both directions, with average harm scores around one on a zero-to-seven scale. This limited test detects no safety degradation. It does not prove robustness to adaptive attacks over several turns. Yash will now examine the wider limitations and our own experiments.
